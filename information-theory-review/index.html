How uncertain?
How wrong?

Entropy measures uncertainty. Cross entropy scores a prediction. KL measures the extra cost of getting the distribution wrong.

~10 minute preview6.1.6 excludedFocus: 6.2.6
The equation to connect it all
Hce(p,q) = H(p) + DKL(p ∥ q)

p = target / data distribution

q = model / approximation

Unavoidable uncertainty + model mismatch = expected prediction cost. Use the same logarithm base throughout.

01 / SECTIONS 6.1.1–6.1.5

Start with surprise.

A likely event is unsurprising; a rare event is surprising. Its information is −log p(x). Entropy is the probability-weighted average of that surprise. Log₂ gives bits; natural log gives nats. Use 0 log 0 = 0.

Entropy: uncertainty in p

H(p) = −Σx p(x) log₂ p(x)

For K possible outcomes: 0 ≤ H ≤ log₂ K. Certainty gives 0; a uniform distribution gives the maximum. A fair coin has 1 bit; a guaranteed heads has 0.

6.1.1 applications: DNA sequence logos use column information 2 − H (four letters), with letter heights proportional to their frequencies. Estimating entropy is difficult with many rare or unseen outcomes.

Cross entropy: score q on data from p

Hce(p,q) = −Σx p(x) log₂ q(x)

Reality supplies the weights p; your model supplies the probabilities inside log q. Confidently assigning a tiny probability to an event that occurs is costly.

Coding interpretation: ideal average code length when using q to encode samples from p. Its minimum is H(p), attained when q = p. Actual finite codes may have overhead.

Joint & conditional entropy

H(X,Y) = −Σx,y p(x,y) log₂ p(x,y)
H(Y|X) = Σx p(x) H(Y|X=x)

Joint: uncertainty about the pair. Conditional: remaining uncertainty in Y after observing X, averaged over X.

  • max{H(X),H(Y)} ≤ H(X,Y) ≤ H(X)+H(Y).
  • Chain rule: H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y|X).
  • For n variables: H(X₁,…,Xₙ)=ΣᵢH(Xᵢ|X₁,…,Xᵢ₋₁).
  • 0 ≤ H(Y|X) ≤ H(Y). Independence gives H(Y|X)=H(Y); deterministic Y=f(X) gives 0.

A particular observation can increase uncertainty; the reduction is guaranteed only on average.

Perplexity: effective number of choices

PP(p) = 2H(p)
PP(data,q) = 2Hce(data,q)

A uniform distribution over K outcomes has perplexity K; certainty has 1. For evaluation, exponentiate the model’s average negative log probability on the observed tokens.

PP = exp[−(1/N) Σᵢ ln q(xᵢ|contextᵢ)]

This is the geometric mean of inverse predictive probabilities. Lower is better on the same evaluation data and tokenization. The population optimum is 2H(p), not H(p).

Try it · a coin and a prediction

p is the real chance of heads. q is your model’s guess. Change q while holding p fixed: only the mismatch changes.

02 / SECTIONS 6.2.1–6.2.5

Mismatch has a price.

DKL(p ∥ q) = Σx p(x) log[p(x)/q(x)] = Hce(p,q) − H(p)

KL is the extra ideal coding cost from using q instead of p. For continuous distributions, replace the sum by an integral: ∫ p(x) log[p(x)/q(x)] dx.

Properties worth knowing

  • KL ≥ 0, and KL = 0 iff the distributions agree (almost everywhere for densities).
  • Generally DKL(p∥q) ≠ DKL(q∥p). It is not a metric: symmetry and the triangle inequality are not guaranteed.
  • If p(x)>0 and q(x)=0, forward KL is infinite. Terms weighted by p(x)=0 contribute zero.

Example in bits: p=(0.8,0.2), q=(0.5,0.5) gives forward KL ≈ 0.278 and reverse KL ≈ 0.322.

6.2.4 · Why is it nonnegative?

Jensen’s inequality says f(E[Z]) ≤ E[f(Z)] for convex f; the direction reverses for concave log. Thus −KL = Ep log(q/p) ≤ log Σx:p(x)>0q(x) ≤ log 1 = 0. Equality requires p=q. Setting q to uniform also gives KL(p∥uniform)=log K−H(p) ≥ 0.

6.2.5 · Why ML uses cross entropy

Hold p fixed. H(p) is constant, so minimizing forward KL is equivalent to minimizing cross entropy. Replace the expectation with an average over training data:

L(θ) = −(1/N) Σᵢ log qθ(xᵢ)

Minimizing this loss is exactly maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). For one correct class y, the classification loss is −log qθ(y|x).

Training samples are only a finite picture of reality: expressive models can overfit. Plausible data augmentation helps represent natural variation. For continuous models, the rigorous connection is an empirical estimate of expected log loss; literal KL from point masses to a continuous density is infinite.

6.2.3 · Gaussian KL

For p=N(μ₁,σ₁²) and q=N(μ₂,σ₂²), using natural logs:

KL(p∥q) = ln(σ₂/σ₁) + [σ₁²+(μ₁−μ₂)²]/(2σ₂²) − ½

Both mean and variance mismatch matter. Swapping the Gaussians changes the denominator and the log ratio. With equal variance σ², KL=(μ₁−μ₂)²/(2σ²).

Multivariate formula
½ [tr(Σ₂⁻¹Σ₁) + (μ₂−μ₁)ᵀΣ₂⁻¹(μ₂−μ₁) − d + ln(det Σ₂ / det Σ₁)]

Here d is the dimension, Σ is a positive-definite covariance matrix, tr adds diagonal entries, and det is the determinant. Result is in nats; divide by ln 2 for bits.

03 / SECTION 6.2.6 · PRIORITY

Which mistakes do you punish?

Keep the target p fixed and fit a simpler q. The distribution before ∥ determines where errors are averaged. A mode is a peak; support is the region with nonzero probability.

04 / ACTIVE RECALL

Close the notes. Explain it back.

Try answering aloud before opening each explanation.

1. Why does a fair coin have more entropy than a biased coin?

Its outcomes are harder to predict. H(0.5)=1 bit, whereas H(0.8)≈0.722 bits. A deterministic coin has 0 bits.

2. What changes when we replace log p with log q?

We move from entropy to cross entropy, but outcomes are still weighted by p. The extra cost is KL(p∥q).

3. Does observing X always reduce uncertainty about Y?

On average, yes: H(Y|X)≤H(Y). One particular observation can increase uncertainty. If Y is determined by X, the conditional entropy is zero.

4. A model’s mean log₂ loss is 3. What is its perplexity?

2³=8: an effective eight-way prediction difficulty. Use the average loss, not the sum.

5. Why is cross-entropy training equivalent to MLE?

Empirical cross entropy is −(1/N)Σ log q(xᵢ). Minimizing it maximizes the log likelihood. The factor 1/N does not change the optimum.

6. Why can reverse KL ignore a real peak?

It averages under q. If q puts almost no mass near a target peak, that region gets almost no weight. Placing q in a low-p valley is expensive, so a restricted q may stay near one peak.

7. Can a KL term be negative even though total KL is nonnegative?

Yes. At outcomes where p(x)<q(x), p(x)log[p(x)/q(x)] is negative. The full probability-weighted sum is nonnegative.
